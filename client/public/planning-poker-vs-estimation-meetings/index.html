Planning Poker vs Open Estimation Meetings

As of October 2026.

Most teams estimate in one of two ways. In an open estimation meeting, the team talks about an item, somebody says a number, and the others agree or push back. In planning poker, each person picks a number in private, and all the cards show at the same moment. Both end with an estimate. The main difference is who speaks first.

The main difference: who speaks first

In an open meeting, the first number pulls the others toward it. This is anchoring bias. When the first number comes from the most senior person, the others often stay quiet, even when they see a risk. The meeting then ends with one number and no record of the doubt.

In planning poker, nobody sees a number before they commit their own. If one person votes 3 and another votes 13, the team learns that they understand the item differently. That gap is the most useful output of the meeting.

When an open meeting is enough

  • One person knows the area, and the others have no basis for a number yet.
  • You size roadmap epics roughly. Here T-shirt sizing is often faster than numbers.
  • Two or three people with deep shared context agree quickly anyway.
  • The items are already split to a similar size, and you only count them.

When planning poker works better

  • There is a seniority gap, and junior members tend to follow the lead.
  • The team meets on video calls, where it is hard to see who disagrees. See planning poker for remote teams.
  • New people joined, and the team does not share one idea of "a 5" yet.
  • Estimates often turn out wrong, and nobody knows why.

Combine them: talk first, then vote

  1. The Product Owner explains the item. The team asks questions, but nobody says a number.
  2. Everybody votes in private, and the cards show at once.
  3. If the cards are close, take the number and move on.
  4. If they are far apart, the highest and the lowest voter explain. Then vote again.
  5. If the spread stays wide, split the item or find more information. Do not force a number.

Common questions

Is planning poker slower than an open discussion?

Not on most items. When the cards agree, the round takes under a minute. The extra time goes to items where the team disagrees, and that is where the talk pays off.

Does planning poker give more accurate estimates?

It removes one known source of error, anchoring, and it shows disagreement early. Accuracy still depends on how well the items are refined.

Do we need a tool for planning poker?

In one room, paper cards work. On a video call, a browser tool shows all votes at the same moment and keeps them hidden until then.

Should we use planning poker for every item?

No. Use it where the team can disagree. For tiny, familiar items, a quick open agreement is enough.
